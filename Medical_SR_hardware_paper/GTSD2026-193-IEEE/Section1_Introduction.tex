\section{Introduction}

Super-resolution (SR) is a difficult approach \cite{nandal2024super}. A low-resolution (LR) image is reconstructed in high-resolution (HR) using SR techniques. Compared to LR images, HR images have more amplified information structures. Image SR, in technical terms, is a method of reconstructing the high-frequency components of LR images. SR techniques have been put out in recent years for both natural and medical images. Medical imaging serves as a vital tool for medical professionals to diagnose and treat patients. For quantitative analysis and accurate diagnosis, isotropic medical images in HR having detailed textural and structural information are required. Small anatomical structures and other minute information in medical imaging might reveal vital information that can be helpful for diagnosis. For instance, in brain MRI, the minute structural characteristics surrounding a tumor aid in the diagnosis of the tumor's origin and rate of growth \cite{tanno2017bayesian, connolly2003tumor}. Similar to this, accurately identifying tiny arteries in retinal images aids in the diagnosis of inflammation of nerves, a sign of hypertensive retinopathy. Obtaining such images in clinical practice is difficult as several factors affect the quality of medical images, including hardware specifications, acquisition times, physiological movement, and the patient's acceptable radiation dose. The upgradation of image-acquiring equipment is not only costly, but it is also limited in its ability to increase the quality of images \cite{yang2023deep}. Therefore, medical images can be post-processed and reconstructed in HR using SR techniques \cite{li2021review} without the need for such an expensive image acquisition approach. Upscaling medical images is a challenge mainly because most of them (CT, MRI, and X-ray) have relatively poor contrast. Secondly, since SR is an ill-posed inverse problem, there are a number of SR outputs for every LR input \cite{bertero1998introduction}. Thirdly, medical image SR does not provide ground truth training datasets like natural image SR does. The primary goal of medical image enhancement efforts has been to enhance the ability to perceive blurry or unclear images visually. The discontinuities of image intensity curves are represented by edges. A strong edge enhancement method is therefore necessary to process these discontinuities in an image.

Over the years, a great deal of research has been done on deep learning-based SR models. Lately, deep learning approaches for SR of images have garnered more attention \cite{hayat2023combined, hayat2024saliency, hayat2024esevsr, nandal2024upscaling, elshafai2023single}. Generally, these models show breakthroughs in performance on different SR benchmarks. SR reconstruction algorithms can be categorized into three divisions, namely, Convolutional Neural Network (CNN)-based methodology (e.g., SRCNN (Super Resolution Convolutional Neural Network) \cite{dong2014learning}, FSRCNN \cite{dong2016accelerating} and ESPCN \cite{shi2016real}), ResNet-based methodology (e.g., VDSR methodology and EDSR methodology \cite{lim2017enhanced}), and GAN-based methodology (e.g., SRGAN \cite{ledig2017photo, xiong2020improved} and DoubleGAN \cite{zhao2022plant}). Fig. 1 displays the classification approaches of SR based on deep learning present in the literature from early techniques employing CNNs (e.g., SRCNN \cite{dong2014learning}) to the current SR methods utilizing transformers (e.g., TTSR \cite{yang2020learning}). However, these methods often entail high computational and storage complexity. While real-time SR can be readily achieved in data centers equipped with high-performance cloud GPUs, performing SR at the edge presents significant challenges due to constrained hardware resources and power limitations \cite{liu2024high}.

To overcome this problem, many researchers have proposed various architectures and techniques to accelerate the inference of CNNs \cite{huynh2022fpga}. With respect to hardware implementations, three hardware platforms can be used as CNN accelerators: graphic processing units (GPU) \cite{strigl2010performance}, application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) \cite{chen2016eyeriss}, and field programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) \cite{zhang2015optimizing, sit2017fpga}. Among these platforms, FPGA has revealed itself as the high-performance and low-cost embedded device very suitable for the hardware prototyping of convolutional accelerators. Recently, new FPGA hardware solutions have been introduced, allowing for an efficient hardware and software co-design framework for deep learning applications. The PYNQ \cite{pynqhomepage} is an open-source project from Xilinx that makes it easier to develop FPGA-based deep learning applications, in which designers can efficiently combine the benefits of programmable logic and microprocessors using the Python language and libraries.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper presents an end-to-end hardware-software co-design framework for accelerating medical image super-resolution on a resource-constrained edge FPGA platform. Specifically, we propose a lightweight 3-layer SRCNN architecture tailored for low-power edge deployment on the Xilinx Zynq-7000 SoC (PYNQ-Z2). To eliminate floating-point arithmetic overhead and comply with the physical constraints of only 220 DSP slices on the target XC7Z020 chip, we apply Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) to compress the model into a signed 8-bit fixed-point (Q7) representation. On the hardware side, an optimized streaming RTL accelerator is designed with line-buffers, parallel MAC units, and an adder tree, seamlessly interfaced with the host ARM processing system via high-throughput AXI4-Stream DMA transfers. Furthermore, an overlap-and-save tiling mechanism is integrated on the host to eliminate boundary blocking artifacts on large medical radiographs. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a hardware-friendly, lightweight 3-layer SRCNN architecture and establish a Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) Q7 scheme that effectively minimizes quantization errors and preserves critical anatomical details in medical images.
    \item We design a fully pipelined RTL convolutional accelerator on the Xilinx Zynq-7000 (XC7Z020) FPGA, achieving high hardware compute efficiency by utilizing on-chip line buffers and adder trees while strictly respecting the 220 DSP budget.
    \item We implement an end-to-end PYNQ-based deployment with AXI DMA streaming and overlap-and-save tiling, validating the system across comprehensive clinical benchmarks (NIH ChestX-ray14 and Pediatric Pneumonia datasets) in terms of reconstruction fidelity, inference latency, and energy efficiency.
\end{itemize}
