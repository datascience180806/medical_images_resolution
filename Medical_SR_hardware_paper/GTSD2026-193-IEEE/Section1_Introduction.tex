\section{Introduction}

Medical imaging modalities, including X-ray radiography, Computed Tomography (CT), and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), serve as indispensable diagnostic cornerstones in modern healthcare. The clinical efficacy of these imaging systems relies heavily on image quality---specifically spatial resolution, contrast, noise characteristics, and artifact suppression---which collectively determine the visibility and interpretability of subtle anatomical and pathological structures \cite{obuchowicz2026image, palma2021review}. However, acquiring high-resolution (HR) images directly during clinical routines is fundamentally constrained by acquisition time, patient motion, radiation exposure, and physical hardware limitations \cite{chen2023deep, shin2024super, occhipinti2026ultra}. For instance, achieving higher spatial resolution in MRI inherently prolongs scan duration and amplifies motion sensitivity \cite{chen2023deep}, whereas X-ray and CT modalities are restricted by radiation dose safety regulations, motivating low-dose acquisition protocols that often introduce significant Poisson-Gaussian noise and degradation artifacts \cite{shin2024super, occhipinti2026ultra}. Upgrading imaging hardware to overcome these physical barriers requires exorbitant capital expenditure while remaining bounded by physical sensor limits.

To bypass these hardware restrictions, Single Image Super-Resolution (SISR) has emerged as an effective post-acquisition computational paradigm, aiming to recover lost high-frequency spatial components and reconstruct high-fidelity HR representations from Low-Resolution (LR) observations \cite{yang2019deep, liu2025comprehensive}. Nevertheless, applying SR to medical modalities presents profound technical challenges. Fundamentally, SISR is an ill-posed inverse problem in which a single LR observation can correspond to multiple plausible HR solutions \cite{yang2019deep, liu2025comprehensive}. Furthermore, medical images often exhibit poor soft-tissue contrast and low signal-to-noise ratios, making unconstrained reconstruction susceptible to noise amplification and boundary artifacts \cite{obuchowicz2026image, varghese2024enhancing}. In particular, this concern is especially pronounced for generative and learned-prior-based reconstruction methods, which have been shown to occasionally introduce anatomically implausible or hallucinated structures \cite{bhadra2021hallucinations, giraldo2024perceptual}. This motivates the use of a mapping-based CNN architecture, which offers more predictable and interpretable reconstruction behavior compared to generative approaches, and is therefore better suited to safety-critical medical imaging applications.

Driven by the rapid evolution of deep learning, learning-based super-resolution frameworks have achieved remarkable improvements in reconstruction fidelity across widely used benchmark evaluations \cite{dong2014learning, kim2016accurate, lim2017enhanced}. In the literature, deep SISR architectures have evolved through several major paradigms. Early pioneering methodologies utilized compact Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to learn non-linear mappings directly from low-resolution inputs to high-resolution outputs, exemplified by SRCNN \cite{dong2014learning}, FSRCNN \cite{dong2016accelerating}, and ESPCN \cite{shi2016real}. While FSRCNN \cite{dong2016accelerating} and ESPCN \cite{shi2016real} reduce theoretical computation by performing feature extraction in low-resolution space and upsampling only at the final layer, their reliance on learned deconvolution or sub-pixel convolution layers introduces additional hardware complexity for streaming FPGA implementations. In contrast, SRCNN's straightforward feed-forward mapping structure is more amenable to a fully pipelined, resource-constrained RTL design, motivating its selection as the architectural basis for this work. To improve representation capability in deeper models, residual learning was subsequently introduced in networks such as VDSR \cite{kim2016accurate} and EDSR \cite{lim2017enhanced}, substantially improving pixel-level reconstruction fidelity. More recently, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), exemplified by SRGAN \cite{ledig2017photo}, have been investigated to recover perceptually realistic high-frequency textures through adversarial and perceptual objectives. In parallel, Vision Transformer-based architectures, such as SwinIR \cite{liang2021swinir}, have demonstrated strong performance for image super-resolution by exploiting long-range feature interactions through hierarchical self-attention.

Despite their impressive reconstruction performance, many high-performing SR models introduce substantial computational, parameter, and memory requirements, creating significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained platforms. This issue is particularly pronounced for embedded and edge devices, where limited computational capability, memory capacity, and power budgets constrain the practical deployment of deep SR networks \cite{wang2023lightweight, xiao2022mfen}. Recent studies on on-device SR further demonstrate that high-resolution inference can impose considerable latency and computational costs, motivating the development of parameter-efficient and hardware-aware architectures \cite{hadji2025edge}. These constraints become even more critical for FPGA-based implementations, where computational complexity, operator efficiency, and memory bandwidth directly affect achievable throughput and hardware resource utilization \cite{huang2026uasr}. Consequently, bridging the gap between high-fidelity SR reconstruction and efficient edge inference remains an important research challenge.

To alleviate these hardware constraints, extensive research has focused on dedicated computing architectures for accelerating deep neural network inference at the edge \cite{sze2017efficient}. From an implementation perspective, hardware acceleration approaches can broadly be realized using three major computing platforms: Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) \cite{krizhevsky2012imagenet}, Application-Specific Integrated Circuits (ASICs) \cite{jouppi2017datacenter}, and Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) \cite{mittal2020survey}. GPUs provide highly parallel computational resources and high throughput, but their general-purpose architecture can incur considerable power and thermal costs under sustained inference workloads. ASICs can achieve highly optimized performance and energy efficiency for targeted workloads, but their fixed hardware architecture limits post-deployment reconfigurability and entails substantial design and fabrication costs. In contrast, FPGAs provide an attractive architectural middle ground, combining fine-grained hardware customization and reconfigurability with the potential for low-latency and energy-efficient inference \cite{sze2017efficient, mittal2020survey}. To facilitate the development of edge-based deep learning applications, the open-source Xilinx PYNQ (Python Productivity for Zynq) framework provides a high-level Python and Jupyter-based programming environment for Zynq-based heterogeneous SoCs. PYNQ exposes FPGA programmable-logic designs as reusable hardware overlays that can be controlled from Python running on the embedded ARM processor, thereby facilitating hardware--software co-design and application-specific hardware acceleration \cite{pynqhomepage, kalapothas2022efficient}.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper presents an end-to-end hardware-software co-design framework for accelerating medical image super-resolution on a resource-constrained edge FPGA platform. Specifically, we propose a compact 3-layer SRCNN architecture ($1 \rightarrow 16 \rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 1$) with 1,624 weights and 25 biases tailored for low-power edge deployment on the Xilinx Zynq-7020 SoC (PYNQ-Z2). To eliminate floating-point arithmetic overhead and comply with the physical constraints of the target XC7Z020 chip, we apply Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) to compress the model into a signed 8-bit fixed-point representation (S7.0 pixel inputs and S0.7 weights, commonly denoted as Q7). On the hardware side, an optimized streaming RTL accelerator is designed with line-buffers, parallel MAC units, and an adder tree, seamlessly interfaced with the host ARM processing system via high-throughput AXI4-Stream DMA transfers. Furthermore, an overlap-and-save tiling mechanism with edge replication padding is integrated on the host to eliminate boundary blocking artifacts on arbitrary medical radiographs. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We propose a hardware-friendly, compact 3-layer SRCNN architecture ($1 \rightarrow 16 \rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 1$) and establish a Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) signed 8-bit fixed-point scheme (S7.0/S0.7) that eliminates arithmetic overflow while maintaining high reconstruction quality with 39.28~dB PSNR and 0.9598 SSIM on clinical radiographs.
    \item We design a fully pipelined, streaming RTL convolutional accelerator on the Xilinx Zynq-7020 FPGA, achieving timing closure at 100~MHz and utilizing only 120 DSP48E1 slices (54.55\%) and 3.5 BRAM blocks (2.50\%) with a low PL dynamic power of only 0.047~W.
    \item We implement an end-to-end PYNQ-based deployment with AXI4-Stream DMA streaming and overlap-tiling, achieving an accelerator throughput of 157.9~patches/s (6.33~ms/patch) and a full-chip power consumption of 1.438~W across 2,100 clinical radiographs from the NIH Chest X-ray benchmark.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews related work on medical image super-resolution algorithms and FPGA-based deep learning accelerators. Section~III details the proposed hardware-software co-design framework, including the PTQ fixed-point quantization scheme and the RTL accelerator architecture. Section~IV presents the experimental results and performance evaluation across objective image quality metrics, hardware resource utilization, and energy efficiency. Finally, Section~V concludes the paper and outlines directions for future research.
